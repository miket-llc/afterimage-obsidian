# Phosphor and Persistence

A long-form reading test. The point of this note is not its subject but its
length: roughly two thousand words of ordinary prose, with the punctuation,
numerals, emphasis and awkward edge cases that real writing produces. If a
typeface survives this page at a comfortable size, it can carry a working
vault. If it does not, no amount of styling elsewhere will rescue it.

## The tube

A cathode-ray tube is a vacuum bottle with an electron gun at the narrow end
and a phosphor-coated screen at the wide one. The gun emits a beam; deflection
coils steer it; the phosphor converts the beam's kinetic energy into visible
light. That is the whole mechanism, and it has not changed in principle since
Ferdinand Braun built the first one in 1897 — only in precision, brightness,
and the number of times per second it can be persuaded to redraw a picture.

What matters for our purposes is the phosphor. It is not a mirror and not a
lamp. It is a material that *stores* energy briefly and releases it as light
over a short but non-zero interval. The interval is called persistence, and
it is why a CRT does not flicker itself into unreadability at sixty frames
per second, and why a bright object dragged across a dark screen leaves a
faint tail behind it. The tail is the afterimage. It is not a defect; it is
the storage medium doing what it was chosen to do.

Persistence is measured as the time for emission to decay to one per cent of
its peak. Short-persistence phosphors — the P22 family used in colour
televisions — decay in well under a millisecond. Long-persistence phosphors
like P39, the yellow-green used in radar displays and in a great many
monochrome terminals, can take tens of milliseconds. The IBM 3270's
characteristic slow, restful green owes as much to that decay curve as to
its hue.

There is a trade. Long persistence smooths flicker and reduces eye strain on
a static page of text, which is exactly what a terminal displays. It also
smears anything that moves. A phosphor chosen for a page of code is a poor
choice for a flight simulator, and vice versa. Terminal manufacturers, who
knew perfectly well that their customers stared at motionless text for eight
hours at a stretch, chose accordingly.

## Why the characters looked the way they did

A text-mode display of the 1980s did not draw letters. It drew a grid of
cells and looked up a bitmap for each one. The IBM VGA's default text mode
was eighty columns by twenty-five rows, and each cell was nine pixels wide by
sixteen tall — though the character bitmaps themselves were eight wide, with
the ninth column generated by the hardware. For most glyphs that ninth column
was blank, which is what gave DOS text its slightly airy inter-character gap.
For the box-drawing characters in the range 0xC0–0xDF, the hardware
replicated the eighth column into the ninth so that horizontal rules joined
up without a seam. It is a small, specific hack, and it is the reason
box-drawing art from that era looks continuous rather than dotted.

The consequence for typography is severe and interesting: every glyph was
built on a grid whose smallest unit was a whole pixel. There were no
fractional stems, no optical corrections, no hinting in the modern sense —
because there was nothing to hint. A stem was one pixel or two. A curve was a
staircase. The designer's entire vocabulary was which cells to fill.

Working inside that constraint produced letterforms with a particular kind of
decisiveness. Look closely at the lowercase 'a' in the IBM VGA 8×16 font:
it is a double-storey 'a', which is ambitious in a five-pixel-wide bowl, and
it works because every pixel is doing structural labour. Nothing is
decorative. Compare it with the same character in a nine-pixel-wide face and
you can see the designer spending the extra column on the counter rather than
on the stem.

## Scanlines are not an overlay

Here is the part that is routinely misunderstood, and it matters for anyone
trying to reproduce the look.

On a real tube, the beam sweeps horizontally. It paints one line, flies back,
drops down, and paints the next. Between those lines, on a display whose
vertical resolution is lower than the tube's capability, there is simply less
excited phosphor — a dimmer band. The visible horizontal striping of a CRT
is therefore *part of how the image is constructed*. It is not a film laid
over a finished picture.

That distinction has a direct visual consequence. If you take a rendered page
of text and lay a striped translucent layer over the top, the stripes pass
across the letters and the background with equal indifference, and the result
looks like exactly what it is: a page with a screen door in front of it. The
letters do not belong to the raster; they are merely behind it. The eye
detects this immediately, even if it cannot say why.

If instead the raster is *inside* the glyph — if the character's own bright
areas are the thing being interrupted, while the background stays whole — the
letter reads as something emitted rather than something printed. The
structure and the type become one object. Achieving that on a real display
was automatic, because there was no other way for the image to exist.
Achieving it in software takes deliberate work.

## Bloom, burn-in, and misregistration

Three further effects distinguish a photograph of a CRT from a screenshot of
a flat panel.

**Bloom.** Bright phosphor scatters light within the glass faceplate. A white
character on black does not end at its own edge; it carries a soft halo a
pixel or two beyond. Crank the brightness and the halo grows until adjacent
strokes merge and the text turns to mush — which is why the brightness knob
on a terminal was a legibility control, not a preference. Restraint is the
whole art here. A little bloom reads as *emission*. Too much reads as *blur*,
and blur is the one thing a page of text cannot afford.

**Burn-in and afterimage.** These are different phenomena often conflated.
True burn-in is permanent damage: phosphor that has been excited in the same
pattern for months loses efficiency, and the ghost of a menu bar remains
visible on a blank screen for the rest of the tube's life. Afterimage, by
contrast, is temporary — the decay tail described earlier. A short afterimage
is felt rather than seen. A long one is a distraction.

**Convergence error.** A colour CRT fires three beams at three phosphors
through a shadow mask. Aligning them perfectly across the whole face of a
curved tube is difficult, and alignment drifts with age, temperature, and the
Earth's magnetic field. The result is chromatic fringing: a red edge on one
side of a white stroke, a blue one on the other. In small amounts it gives
white text a faint iridescence that photographs beautifully. In large amounts
it is simply broken.

## What this means for a text editor

A knowledge tool is not a demo. It is a surface a person looks at for six or
eight hours, mostly while reading and typing, and the honest test of any
visual treatment is whether it survives the ten-thousandth minute rather than
the first.

That argues for a specific discipline:

1. **Sharpness is not negotiable.** Whatever is done to the glyphs, the
   punctuation must stay unambiguous. A comma and a full stop must remain
   distinguishable at a glance; an apostrophe must not dissolve into its own
   halo. If the effect softens the type, the effect is wrong.
2. **Effects belong on the display layer, not the editing layer.** Anything
   that interferes with the caret, with text selection, with a search match,
   or with an input method's composition preview is a defect no matter how
   good it looks in a screenshot.
3. **Motion is a garnish.** A brief resolve when a note opens is pleasant. A
   permanent scanning beam is a migraine. Nothing should move while a person
   is typing.
4. **The look must survive stillness.** If the personality of an interface
   depends on animation, it has no personality — it has a screensaver. The
   test is a static screenshot at twenty per cent scale: is the thing still
   recognisably itself?

## A note on measurement

Some numbers worth keeping in mind, because they recur:

| Quantity | Typical value |
|---|---|
| VGA text cell | 9 × 16 px (8 × 16 bitmap) |
| VGA text mode | 80 × 25 characters |
| Refresh rate | 60–72 Hz |
| P39 persistence | ~40 ms to 1 % |
| Shadow-mask pitch | 0.25–0.31 mm |

The 16-pixel cell height is the useful one. A font built on a 16-pixel grid
renders at exactly one font pixel per CSS pixel when set at 16 px, and at
exactly two device pixels per font pixel on a 2× display. Every horizontal
band lands on a boundary; nothing falls between rows and turns grey. Set the
same font at 15 px or 17 px and the grid no longer divides evenly, the
renderer resamples, and the crispness that justified choosing a pixel font in
the first place is thrown away. It is a rare case where the "correct" value is
genuinely a single number rather than a matter of taste.

## Coda

There is a temptation, when reviving an old look, to reach for the most
legible symptoms — the curved corners, the sweeping beam, the green-on-black
— and to assemble them into a costume. The result is recognisable and
completely dead, because the symptoms were never the point. What made these
displays pleasant to work at was a coherence between the type, the light and
the surface: a set of constraints that produced a particular kind of clarity
because there was no way to produce any other kind.

The useful move is not to imitate the artefacts but to adopt the discipline —
decisive letterforms, a fixed grid, restraint with light, and the assumption
that a person is going to read this for a very long time.

That is a harder brief than a scanline overlay, and a much better one.
