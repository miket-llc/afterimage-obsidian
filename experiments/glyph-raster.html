Afterimage — glyph-raster spike

Comparison rig for CRT glyph rendering. The question this page answers is not “can we draw scanlines” but can the glyphs themselves participate in the raster — while remaining sharp, selectable and editable. Methods are tagged EDIT-SAFE (usable in the editor), STATIC ONLY (display text only) or REJECTED. Open at 100% zoom on a 2× display; then use the 20% rig at the foot.

A

Baseline — no treatment

EDIT-SAFE

The control. Everything below is judged against this for sharpness. WebPlus IBM VGA 9x16 at 16px/24px.

B

Page-wide scanline overlay

REJECTED

The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog — “curly quotes”, an apostrophe’s tail, an ellipsis… and 0123456789.

A striped film in normal blend over the whole card. The stripes cross glyph and background identically, so the text reads as sitting behind a screen door. This is the failure mode the brief warns about. Note also that it dims the background as much as the type, costing contrast for nothing.

C

Multiply raster (dark surface)

EDIT-SAFE

The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog — “curly quotes”, an apostrophe’s tail, an ellipsis… and 0123456789.

Muted secondary text, to check the raster does not destroy the faint tokens.

The key method. Same stripes, but mix-blend-mode: multiply. Multiply is proportional, so on a near-black background it subtracts ~3/255 from the surface and ~66/255 from the glyphs: the raster appears only where light is emitted. Physically correct — the dark gap between scan lines can only remove light that phosphor actually emitted. The text element is never touched, so caret, selection, IME and search highlighting are all intact (rig J).

D

background-clip: text

STATIC ONLY

The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog — “curly quotes”, an apostrophe’s tail, an ellipsis… and 0123456789.

Strongest fidelity: the gradient is painted into the letterform and works on any surface colour. But color: transparent makes the caret invisible and lets the selection highlight paint straight through the glyphs. Display text only — never the editor. Try selecting this line and compare with C.

E

C + bloom + afterimage

EDIT-SAFE

The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog — “curly quotes”, an apostrophe’s tail, an ellipsis… and 0123456789.

Bloom pushed too far — punctuation dissolves into its own halo. Rejected.

Bloom = a soft same-colour halo (scattering in the faceplate). Ghost = a dim 1px offset copy (phosphor decay). Both are text-shadow: no layout cost, no compositing layer. The second line shows the failure boundary — beyond ~6px blur the commas and full stops stop being distinguishable.

F

Chromatic misregistration

EDIT-SAFE

Convergence error at 0.4px — a faint iridescence on white strokes.

At 1.2px it reads as a printing fault, not a tube. Rejected.

Sub-pixel only. The default ships at 0 and the slider tops out well before the second line’s strength.

G

Heading plate — one display object

STATIC ONLY
AFTERIMAGE
Heading that wraps onto a second line to prove the plate grows

Two cooperating rasters on a shared grid: the plate is a light substrate so a dark stripe shows on it; the ink is dark so its clipped raster must lighten to be visible. Both are anchored to the plate’s top edge with the same period, so the bands run continuously across substrate and letterform — which is what makes them read as one physical object rather than type on a box. The offset cobalt block behind the first plate is the registration ghost.

H

Light surface — screen raster

LIGHT

On paper the ink is the dark thing, so the raster must lighten in bands rather than darken.

Same principle, inverted: mix-blend-mode: screen lifts dark pixels and barely touches a light page. Light mode ships at roughly half the dark-mode strength — paper does not glow, and a strong raster on white reads as a fault.

I

High-DPI integer alignment

WebPlus IBM VGA has unitsPerEm = 1600 over a 16-pixel cell — exactly 100 units per pixel row. At 16px one font pixel is one CSS pixel (two device pixels at 2×), so every raster band lands on a boundary. At 15 or 17px the grid no longer divides and the renderer resamples: stems go grey and the crispness that justified a pixel font is thrown away. The IBM DOS preset therefore snaps to 16px and its multiples. The 3270 and Typewriter presets are outline fonts and scale freely.

All four at 16px/24px under the same raster. 9x16 is the body default: 8x16’s half-em advance is punishingly tight for long prose, and the extra column buys counter space where it matters most.

L

Emphasis without a bold face

SOLVED

Normal weight at rest, slightly below full brightness.

Bold — full brightness plus bloom, not a heavier glyph.

Italic — synthetic oblique, tinted cooler.

Alt bold — the DOS trick: a 1px horizontal copy.

The problem: IBM VGA and 3270 ship a single weight — no bold, no italic — and synthetic bold smears a pixel font. The fix is period-correct: on a real terminal “bold” was brighter, not fatter. Afterimage renders body text a little below full brightness so bold has somewhere to go, and reserves pure white plus bloom for it. Italic falls back to synthetic oblique with a cool tint. Bureau already treated bold as a graphical stamp, so this inherits cleanly.

Conclusion. Ship C (multiply raster) as the universal, edit-safe base — it is the only method that makes glyphs participate in the raster without touching the text element. Layer E (bloom + afterimage) and optionally F on top. Use D/G for display text only, where the plate and its lettering must fuse into one object. Invert to H on light surfaces. Snap the pixel font to 16px multiples per I. Solve emphasis with brightness per L, not with a synthetic bold face.
